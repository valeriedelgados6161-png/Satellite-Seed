Bridging NASA Data with the Ground Truth of Azuero

Satellite Seed turns NASA climate records into crop-rotation options for smallholder farmers in Panama’s Arco Seco, then checks every option against what farmers actually live in the field.

See how it works Read the method

In the Arco Seco, the dry season shapes every planting

Azuero’s Arco Seco is one of Panama’s driest regions. Small farmers there decide what to plant and when using experience passed down and tested season after season.

Meanwhile, NASA has decades of rainfall and temperature records for every plot of land. But they sit in technical archives built for scientists, not for someone deciding on a Tuesday morning whether to rotate a field.

The data exists. It just does not reach the people who need it most.

From a pin on the map to a clear next step

A farmer should not need to understand a satellite to benefit from one. Satellite Seed does the translation.

  1. Mark the plot

    The farmer drops a pin on the map and says what they grow now and what matters most: saving water, income, or soil health.

  2. Pull NASA’s climate record

    The coordinates go to NASA POWER, which returns years of daily rainfall and temperature for that exact spot.

  3. Add what the farmer knows

    When the rains really started last year, which plots dry out first. Field knowledge sits next to the satellite record.

  4. See two or three options

    Each rotation shows the rule behind it and a confidence label, so the farmer can agree, adjust, or ignore it.

Space data, checked on the ground

Satellites measure wide areas from far away. A farmer sees one field up close. Both can be right and still disagree. Satellite Seed puts them side by side and says how far apart they are.

How we check

  • Interviews with local producers, with their consent, using the same questions for each.
  • Their answers are compared with the NASA record for the same place and period.
  • Every figure is labeled by origin: NASA, farmer, or assumption.
  • Rotation rules are reviewed by an agronomist or experienced producer before use.

Built to be simple on purpose

This is a lean prototype made for the 48 hours of the hackathon. Here is exactly what it is and what it is not.

Prototype
Python application built during NASA Space Apps 2026.
NASA data
NASA POWER daily rainfall and temperature, an open service that needs no account.
Interface
Mobile-first and written in plain language. Farmers get Spanish; reviewers get English summaries.
Transparency
Rules are visible, and every figure shows where it came from.
Limits
It supports a farmer’s decision. It does not replace an agronomist, and it has not yet been tested at scale.

Where Satellite Seed goes next

A rotation choice is a small decision. Across thousands of plots, small decisions add up to food security and climate resilience.

  • More crops and plotsRules validated with agronomists and local farming organizations.
  • Richer NASA layersSoil moisture and satellite rainfall added to the climate record.
  • A community ground-truth networkSimple rain gauges and sensors that feed local measurements back into the tool.
  • Works where connection is weakA Spanish-first mobile experience designed for limited internet.
